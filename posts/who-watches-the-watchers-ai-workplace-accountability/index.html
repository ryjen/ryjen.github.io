Who Watches the Watchers? Trust and Accountability in Software Development

By Robert Ryan Jennings Published Sep 22, 2026 12:00AM 15 min read

software-engineering ai ethics governance

I left an earlier startup environment partly because I feared its people, workload, and working conditions would lead me to burnout. I burnt out anyway while balancing engineering work with part-time schooling. My life outside work was changing, too: I had been through a breakup, and family relationships and responsibilities were shifting. I mention this as context for my own capacity at the time, not as an explanation of anyone else’s conduct at work.

When I returned, the workplace had become an enterprise environment rather than the organization I had known. I had lost my stake in the work—my sense of ownership and influence—and the leadership role I previously held. I was now working under leaders whose decisions affected my contribution and career, and over time I found I could no longer trust them.

I wanted to move to a team where I could rebuild a workable professional relationship. That is the context for everything that followed: the fundamental problem was trust.

I have never been the kind of developer who is content to coast as long as management is happy. I want to contribute meaningfully: understand the system, solve difficult problems, and do work I can stand behind. That drive has also come with a developer’s ego. I can become too attached to my own technical judgment. In the age of AI, I am still learning to separate confidence in my work from the need to be right. Burnout changed my relationship with the work, too. I cannot pretend that my own expectations and reactions played no part in the conflict.

I had also been naive about the industry in a particular way. I knew how to build software, but I had not always paid enough attention to the commercial incentives, organizational authority, and professional relationships that shape who gets to make decisions and whose work is recognized. I expected competence, contribution, and good faith to carry more weight than they sometimes do. That does not mean I now know what anyone was privately thinking or that every disagreement concealed an agenda. It means technical ability alone had not prepared me to navigate the organization I returned to.

There has also long been a gap between my practical engineering experience and my formal education. I developed much of my technical judgment by doing the work while continuing to study part-time. Navigating the expectations attached to credentials, seniority, and hands-on competence was difficult enough; managing disagreements about them while burnt out was harder. I could be confident in a technical position and still struggle to explain it, accept a different decision, or ask for support without feeling that my professional value was being questioned. That is my part of the relationship to examine, not proof of what anyone else thought about my education or skills.

That is part of why trust matters. I needed room to disagree, to be wrong, to be corrected, and to correct others without every disagreement becoming a judgment about character or professional value. An organization must be able to challenge an engineer’s work; an engineer must be able to challenge the organization’s conclusions.

At the time, I had the impression that I was being pushed to ask for help. I could not establish whether that was anyone’s intention. The irony, as I experienced it, was that nobody was plainly asking me what help I needed or what would make the working relationship function. An indirect expectation to ask for help is especially hard to navigate when asking requires trust in the person expected to provide it.

I also regarded some of the leadership’s technical judgment as weaker than my own. That was my assessment, not an objective measure of anyone’s skill, and I can see how my developer ego may have made collaboration harder. Neither my assessment of their skills nor my earlier concern about burnout tells me what anyone intended; they do help explain why appeals to authority or vague offers of help did not reassure me.

Looking back, I wonder whether my departure from the earlier startup was misunderstood as opposition to new management. I was not trying to join a faction or act on behalf of any workplace alliance. I was making a decision about the people, work, and conditions I thought I could manage. I do not know whether anyone actually interpreted it otherwise. But this is exactly the kind of ambiguity that should be addressed through direct communication, rather than letting assumptions about loyalty stand in for an employee’s stated reasons.

I also wondered whether my circumstances were being read as desperation, and whether that might have made it easier for someone to pressure me into accepting terms I would otherwise question. I do not know if anyone made that assumption or tried to exploit it. But the possibility sharpened my sense of the power imbalance: needing a job, support, or a workable team should not reduce an employee’s ability to ask for clear expectations and fair treatment. I was also worried about damage to my professional reputation that I felt unable to assess or control.

Instead, I found myself in a performance improvement process whose expectations I considered unreasonable and a self defense mechanism for changing teams. Communication about my work felt effectively cut off, and I believed I had little practical ability to continue in the existing role, while the employment relationship itself remained unresolved. I felt under pressure, without a credible way to restore trust or bring the conflict to a close.

Eventually, I did something that I knew could end the situation myself. I sent an email to someone who was not central to the dispute, using fictional UX personas to express my frustration. I called the character representing the person or archetype I felt was pushing me out “ZHUUL” and included a benign single-mother manager persona for the recipient. I intended the caricatures as satire, not a threat or an accusation of actual misconduct. Looking back, I also wonder whether sending it was an indirect attempt to communicate how isolated and unsupported I felt. That is my interpretation of my own action, not evidence of anyone else’s intentions.

I saw the email as relatively harmless in content, but I knew it could damage my employment situation. I felt I had been left to bring about my own departure when I could no longer find a workable path forward. Sending it was still my decision, and I take responsibility for involving someone who was not at the center of the disagreement. My intention and the recipient’s experience of the email need not have been the same.

I cannot establish that leadership intended me to leave this way or forced my decision. But my decision does not settle the questions about the unresolved conflict and the process that preceded it. Neither an employee’s mistake nor a management decision should become an all-purpose explanation for everything that came before.

The central problem was not that I wanted to escape scrutiny. It was that trust had broken down, yet I was expected to accept decisions made through processes I did not feel able to challenge.

What remains is a question that extends beyond my own experience: How can an employee challenge an organizational decision when the people making it also control much of the evidence used to justify it?

Software engineers already understand why trust alone is an inadequate security boundary. We use attributable identities, audit logs, separation of duties, and independent verification. Why should the exercise of authority over the people building those systems receive less scrutiny?

Monitoring is not the problem; unaccountable monitoring is

I do not object to workplace monitoring when it has a legitimate, clearly communicated purpose and is governed ethically. I regard myself as a competent and ethical engineer, and I am willing to have my work examined, including mistakes and difficult decisions. Monitoring can help identify technical bottlenecks, reveal communication gaps, and show where management could provide better direction, resources, or support. If both sides can examine the same evidence, it can improve how a team works together.

My objection begins when I no longer trust the people controlling the monitoring. A manager with access to detailed activity records can potentially select incidents out of context, use privileged knowledge for personal or professional advantage, or credit preferred colleagues while discrediting someone else. These are risks of unchecked access, not claims that I can establish any particular manager did those things to me.

The difference is not whether I have something to hide. It is whether the people watching me are themselves accountable for what they collect, how they interpret it, and what they do with it. An employee’s willingness to be monitored should not require blind trust in leadership.

Ethical monitoring should make both engineering work and the exercise of management authority easier to examine. It should not give one side an unanswerable advantage over the other.

Distrust is not itself dishonesty

I disputed the characterization of me as the untrustworthy party. From my perspective, I had lost trust in the working relationship and wanted the expectations and disagreements addressed explicitly.

An employer may have legitimate concerns about performance or conduct even when an employee disputes its assessment. A requested team transfer may not be possible. But neither disagreement nor distrust should stand in for a specific, documented deficiency.

A fair process can explain why reassignment is unavailable, ask directly what support is needed and from whom, distinguish workload and interpersonal conflict from performance, and define achievable expectations the employee can challenge on factual grounds. Burnout may call for workload changes or support; a technical defect calls for diagnosis; a conduct issue calls for its own assessment. They can overlap without being the same problem.

The email and its context should be assessed on their actual content and circumstances, not inflated into something it was not or used to rewrite every preceding disagreement as proof of my dishonesty. My responsibility for sending it does not establish that the organization caused or intended my decision, either.

When trust has broken down, neither side’s account should become fact merely because one side has more authority.

An implementation defect is not the whole incident

On another project, I worked on a client-specific SDK integration built on an existing framework. The integration later encountered serious problems reported after a conference. There was a coding problem in work I performed, and I do not want to obscure my responsibility for it.

I also came away questioning whether the project had received oversight and testing proportionate to its scope. That is my assessment of the experience, not a finding that anyone else deliberately withheld support or caused the failure. I do not want to present this incident as proof of the later conflict with leadership; they are distinct experiences.

A useful review would separate the implementation defect from the wider delivery system: what requirements were documented, what the framework was assumed to guarantee, which integration scenarios were tested, what reviews were performed, and which risks were accepted before release. Those questions can reveal an individual error and a process gap in the same incident.

That is also where trust matters. An engineer needs to be able to admit a mistake without believing the admission will be used to explain away every organizational contribution to the outcome. Management needs an accurate account of the engineer’s actions, rather than an account that automatically shifts responsibility elsewhere. Neither side benefits from a review whose conclusion is decided before the evidence is examined.

When the organization controls the evidence

The asymmetry is particularly stark in software development. Teams administer the shared environments that produce build results, review work, control access to project records, and interpret incidents. Those are legitimate operational functions. Combining them with unchecked authority over performance assessments creates a risk of unfair attribution.

At a previous workplace, I once saw a cursor move unexpectedly on a shared Macintosh used for continuous integration. I did not investigate it and have no record establishing why it moved. Remote administration, another input device, automation, and a technical fault are all possible explanations. I cannot infer covert monitoring or sabotage from that observation.

The useful question is narrower: if a shared machine produces a result used to judge an engineer’s work, can the organization reconstruct relevant changes and privileged access? If the records are incomplete, can it admit that responsibility is unresolved rather than assigning it to the engineer nearest the failure?

Monitoring introduces another asymmetry. Between assignments, I sometimes spoke aloud while using my terminal or practiced with a typing tutor. When my relationship with leadership deteriorated, I wondered whether incidental activity might have been observed and misinterpreted. I have no record that anyone collected or used it. Other people’s reactions cannot establish that they had access to it.

The general concern does not require that inference to be true: telemetry collected to secure infrastructure should not silently become unrestricted material for employee profiling or professional advantage. Access should be purpose-limited and auditable, with employees able to understand the monitoring that affects them and challenge material factual errors. The same records that document an engineer’s mistakes may also reveal inadequate management support or failures of communication; oversight must permit those findings as well.

I also worried after leaving that the conflict might affect my references. I received no documented communication establishing that unfavorable information was shared, and cannot attribute later professional interactions to my former employer. Still, an employee may lose access to the records behind a disputed assessment while the employer retains them. Accurate, relevant employment disclosures and contestable records matter independently of my uncertainty.

The same principle applies to engineering credit. Shared work benefits from reuse and collaboration, but an employer’s ownership of code is not the same thing as accurate recognition of who designed, implemented, reviewed, or improved it. I cannot establish that anyone deliberately took credit for my contributions. A reviewable project history is nonetheless preferable to an assessment based only on whoever controls the narrative.

What AI adds—and what it does not

Conventional logging, version control, and deterministic policy checks should establish the authoritative record. AI cannot make a missing or manipulated log trustworthy. Its narrower value is helping people examine dispersed records: correlating events across systems, retrieving relevant context, surfacing contradictions, and making the limits of the evidence legible.

Consider a hypothetical engineer criticized for three failed CI releases. They argue that the shared runner changed during the same period.

An independently governed accountability process could work as follows:

  1. Define a bounded case. Record the disputed assessment, time window, authorized purpose, access permissions, and retention period. Exclude unrelated employee activity.
  2. Preserve the sources. Collect build IDs, commits, reviews, runner-image changes, authenticated administration events, incident records, and documented delivery expectations. Retain provenance and integrity metadata rather than replacing evidence with an AI summary.
  3. Use AI to inspect, not rule. Correlate the timeline and flag candidate relationships. Suppose two failures followed an infrastructure change while the third is reproducibly caused by an implementation defect. The timing alone does not prove the change caused the first two failures, much less that anyone made it maliciously.
  4. Allow challenge. The engineer and management can each identify missing records, challenge factual errors, and supply context. Their accounts remain separately attributed.
  5. Require independent review. A reviewer who does not unilaterally control the runner or the disputed assessment examines the underlying evidence and documents which conclusions it supports, which remain uncertain, and how the performance decision follows.

This does not automatically exonerate the engineer or accuse an administrator. It prevents three superficially similar failures from being treated as one person’s fault without examining their distinct causes.

The boundary matters: AI can help interpret evidence, but it must not control the evidence, grant itself authority, or make the final employment decision.

What we lose when AI removes the struggle

Some of my most difficult experiences have also forced me to grow. I have had to reckon with burnout, the limits of my technical confidence, and how hard it can be to navigate conflict when I do not trust the people involved. I would not choose those experiences as a teaching method for anyone else, but I do not want to outsource what I can learn from them.

AI makes it tempting to skip the uncomfortable work: have it decide who was right, draft the hard conversation, or supply a convincing explanation before I have examined my own part in what happened. That may reduce friction while also removing the opportunity to develop judgment, humility, and the ability to disagree constructively. A polished answer is not the same as an earned understanding.

The point of using AI here is not to spare anyone from accountability or from the work of reflection. It is to make relevant evidence accessible enough that people can confront it, challenge it, and make their own decisions. Support should not become substitution.

Who governs the AI?

An AI system controlled exclusively by the same authority it is meant to scrutinize could make the original problem worse. It could join sensitive records across purposes, generate a persuasive story from incomplete information, and make an unjustified assessment appear objective.

The safeguards therefore have to precede the model: least-privilege, case-specific access; verifiable provenance; auditable administrator and investigator actions; separation of duties; retention limits; an opportunity to contest factual conclusions; and independent human review. No secret character scoring, automatic discipline, or inference of hidden motives from incidental behavior.

These controls protect management as well as employees. A manager can demonstrate that expectations were explicit and a decision was supported by evidence. An employee can challenge a technical attribution without first having to convince the organization that they are personally trustworthy.

AI cannot restore a failed relationship or determine what someone secretly intended. It can help an accountable process distinguish observation from interpretation and uncertainty from fact.

The fundamental issue was trust. The answer is not to demand more of it from the party with less authority.

An ethical organization should be able to demonstrate that a decision was fair even when the employee no longer trusts the people who made it.
